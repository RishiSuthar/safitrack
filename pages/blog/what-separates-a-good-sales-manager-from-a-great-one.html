What separates a good sales manager from a great one

The gap between good and great management in field sales isn't about harder work or better scripts. It's about where you focus your attention.

SMSadie F., SafiTrack

The managers who get called "great" by their teams tend to share a few habits that their merely good counterparts don't. None of these habits are secret. Most are things every manager would say they agree with in principle. The difference is in the doing.

They manage the activity, not just the outcome

Every sales manager cares about numbers. Great managers understand that the number is a lagging indicator. By the time it shows up in the monthly report, the window to influence it has already closed. What you can influence is the activity: visits made, calls logged, follow-ups sent, pipeline reviewed. A manager who is watching activity weekly can intervene when patterns break. A manager who only watches revenue is always three months behind the problem.

This sounds obvious until you're in the middle of a quarter with a full calendar and the easiest version of the job is to check in on the big deals and let everything else run. The discipline to look at activity data for every rep, every week, even when the numbers are green. That's what separates the managers who build durable teams from the ones who inherit good reps and coast.

They invest time in the middle of the distribution

A common mistake is concentrating all coaching energy on either the bottom of the team (the reps at risk of a PIP) or the top (the star performers you're trying to keep happy). The most leverage, almost always, is in the middle. The competent reps who are performing adequately but who have room to move meaningfully if someone actually works with them.

A rep who moves from adequate to good represents a larger absolute gain than a struggling rep who moves from poor to adequate. Middle-of-distribution coaching is where the team's overall performance shifts, but it requires patience and the willingness to invest time in people who don't feel urgent.

They give feedback in the field, not in the office

Most sales feedback happens in a meeting room, from notes taken two days after a visit the manager heard about secondhand. This is almost useless. The feedback that actually changes behaviour is specific, immediate, and observed firsthand. Great managers spend time in the field with reps. Not to take over, but to watch, and then debrief in the car immediately after.

"You lost the room when you led with pricing. They hadn't agreed on value yet. Let's talk about what you could have done at that moment instead."

That feedback, given in the car park twenty minutes after it happened, lands differently than the same observation delivered in a weekly one-to-one. The memory is shared, the context is live, and the rep hasn't had time to reconstruct a narrative that protects their ego.

They know when to get out of the way

The instinct to help (to jump in on a deal, to take a meeting alongside a rep, to add your own suggestions to the proposal) is natural for managers who were previously good individual contributors. It's also frequently counterproductive. Reps who always have a manager to catch their mistakes don't develop the judgment to handle those situations alone. And customers who deal with the manager directly never fully commit to the relationship with the rep.

The best managers I've observed treat their involvement in a deal as a cost, not a feature. Sometimes the cost is worth paying. More often, the better move is to coach the rep before the meeting, let them run it, and debrief after. The rep learns more, the customer relationship stays where it belongs, and the manager's time isn't a bottleneck.

They say the hard thing early

Performance problems almost always have a long visible run-up before anyone says anything formal. The manager notices the pattern, decides to give it more time, hopes it self-corrects. It rarely does. By the time the formal conversation happens, months of context have accumulated and the rep feels ambushed because the feedback they're receiving doesn't match the informal signals they've been getting.

Great managers have the uncomfortable conversation three months earlier, when the pattern is just emerging. That conversation is harder in the moment and almost always better for everyone in the long run. The rep gets a real chance to course-correct. The manager gets to manage rather than document.
