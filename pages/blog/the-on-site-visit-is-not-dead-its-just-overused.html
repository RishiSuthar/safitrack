The on-site visit is not dead. It's just overused.

Remote selling has changed what field visits are for. The teams getting the most from them have changed how they think about when to show up in person.

RSRishi S., SafiTrack

The default assumption in field sales for decades was simple: more visits are better. Show up often enough, the relationship deepens, the deal closes, the account stays. And for a long time, with limited alternatives, this was more or less true.

Remote tools have changed the calculus. A customer who used to require a physical presence to get fifteen minutes with can now have a thirty-minute video call that's more convenient for both parties. The visit is no longer the only way to maintain contact. Which means the visit needs to earn its place rather than simply filling a cadence requirement.

What visits are actually good for

The research on in-person versus remote interaction consistently shows the same thing: physical presence is most valuable when the interaction requires trust-building, navigating complexity, or reading a room. It's less valuable (and often not worth the travel time) for information exchange, status updates, or routine check-ins that have no decision attached.

Translated to field sales: an on-site visit earns its cost when you're meeting someone new in the buying group, when there's a genuine problem to understand firsthand, when you're trying to close something that has stalled, or when the relationship is at risk. It doesn't earn its cost when you're delivering an update the customer could have read in an email or maintaining visibility for its own sake.

The cadence trap

Most field sales organisations set visit cadences by account tier: tier one accounts get monthly visits, tier two quarterly, and so on. This is administratively clean and genuinely useful as a baseline. The problem is when cadence becomes the objective rather than the mechanism.

A rep who is visiting a tier one account monthly because the cadence says to (even when there's nothing to discuss, no decision pending, no relationship repair needed) is burning hours that could go to genuine opportunities. The customer notices too. There is a real phenomenon of "visit fatigue" with over-serviced accounts where the rep becomes background noise.

A better framing Instead of asking "how often should we visit this account?", ask "what has to be true for a visit to this account to be worth doing?" The answer is usually: something has changed, something is at risk, or something is close to a decision. When none of those are true, a phone call or a brief message is often a better use of everyone's time.

What the data shows

Looking at SafiTrack data across our customer base, we see a consistent pattern: visits that are triggered by a specific event (a customer inquiry, a deal stage change, a renewal window opening) convert to outcomes at roughly twice the rate of visits made purely to fulfil a cadence. The event-triggered visits are also shorter on average, because both parties came with something specific to discuss.

The teams with the highest sales efficiency metrics aren't the ones visiting most frequently. They're the ones visiting most purposefully. With a clear reason for being there that the customer would agree with if asked.

How to shift the culture

This is harder than it sounds because "make more visits" is a deeply ingrained management heuristic. Managers who have always measured activity by visit count need new proxies for effort and engagement. The shift is from measuring visits as an input to measuring visit quality as an output. Which requires reps to log what happened and what the next step is, not just that they showed up.

The reps who adapt to this model quickly tend to find it liberating. They spend less time in cars and more time on conversations that are actually going somewhere. The reps who struggle are usually the ones who were using high visit frequency to avoid the harder work of qualification and follow-through.
